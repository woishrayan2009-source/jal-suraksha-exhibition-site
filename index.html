THE PROBLEM

National forecasting knows the river. Nobody watches your drain.

India's flood-forecasting systems operate at river-basin scale — they can tell a district that a river will crest in the next day, but they were never designed to sense that one specific street, basement, or underpass is waterlogging right now. That gap is where sudden, localized flooding actually catches people unaware: the water rises street by street, hours or even minutes before it becomes a basin-wide event anyone official is tracking.

HOW IT WORKS

One reference point, three steps, one alert

STEP 01

Measure Water Level Continuously

An ultrasonic sensor mounted above a fixed reference height — a drain, a basement entrance, a low road point — takes a distance reading on a steady interval, day and night.

STEP 02

Calculate Rate-of-Rise

Each new reading is compared against recent history to compute how fast the level is climbing, not just how high it is — the same rate-of-rise logic Extingo already uses for its heat sensor.

STEP 03

Alert Before Critical Threshold

When the projected rise crosses the safety threshold before it's actually reached, a local alarm fires and an SMS goes out — reported into the Kavach dashboard the same way every other module escalates.

Same Algorithm, New Hazard

Jal Suraksha doesn't introduce a new detection concept — it reapplies one. The rate-of-rise calculation that lets Extingo flag a fire before the temperature alone would trigger an alarm is the exact same pattern used here on a water-level reading instead of a heat reading. One idea, now proven across two hazards, is the thread that runs through Kavach.
